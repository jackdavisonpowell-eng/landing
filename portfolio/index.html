What AUTOGOD is

01

AUTOGOD is an autonomous agent system I run on self-hosted LLM inference — repurposed data-center GPUs doing the work cloud APIs would otherwise bill me for. It watches for useful work, plans it, does it, and hands the result back. AUTOGOD has been through and will go through many iterations; he works 24/7 to improve himself, leaving zero idle GPU hours. This site, the showcase page, and the fleet control panel are all part of the same project.

Everything on the showcase page was built this way — free to run, with no usage limits, because it's my own hardware.

Childhood tinkering

I got obsessed with overclocking. Not the safe kind — I wanted to know exactly where a machine fell over, so I would push the clocks until it did, then back off one notch and live there. You learn a lot about what a component actually is from the point where it stops working.

Alongside that I was making YouTube videos: Blender animations built around the games I was playing, and PC building content. Rendering is the fastest way to find out whether your overclock was really stable.

And I tuned Windows installs for anybody who would let me near their computer. Half of that was genuine optimisation and half was an excuse to take someone else's machine apart.

Discovering what AI could actually do

I started using ChatGPT to help me code in high school, back when that meant copying code out of a chat window and pasting it into an editor yourself. There was no agent, nothing that could type into a terminal on your behalf.

Then the harnesses arrived — Claude Code and the things like it — and the shape of the work changed completely. Something with the expertise of a master's degree could build a real project many times faster than I could by hand. My throughput exploded. Projects that would have been months of grinding stopped being about endurance at all. What was left was ideas, and money.

Money was the wall I kept hitting. Usage limits, constantly. So I went looking for the cheapest way to own the compute instead of renting it, and found old data-center cards with a lot of HBM2 on them going for almost nothing — seventy or eighty dollars for a Tesla P100, because at the time almost nobody wanted one.

Those cards cost considerably more now. Starting early did not just get me cheap hardware, it got me a couple of years of experience with it that I could not buy today at any price.

The networking phase

Once there was more than one server, one flat network stopped being acceptable. I split the house into VLANs: the machines I actually trust, the servers, the IoT things that phone home constantly, and a guest network for everyone else.

Every device on the network resolves DNS through Pi-hole on my own hardware, so ads and telemetry get dropped before they leave the house rather than after somebody else has already logged them.

Then firewall rules decide who may talk to whom. The smart plugs cannot see the servers, guests cannot see anything, and the devices with the worst security are the ones with the least reach. That principle is the whole reason the rest of this stack runs at home instead of in somebody's cloud.

15

Three cards, and the BIOS that would not allow it

Fitting a third card to a Gigabyte X99 board is an address-space problem, not a slot problem. Three compute cards need their memory mapped above the 4 GB line, and on that board the stock firmware's Above 4G Decoding toggle is broken — switch it on and the machine will not boot.

The fix was a modded BIOS from the Win-Raid community that raises the high memory window from 256 GB to 1 TB, flashed off a FreeDOS stick with a patched tool because the stock one rejects a modified image outright. Then Above 4G on, PCIe forced to Gen 3 rather than Auto, legacy video off, and one cleared CMOS after my first attempt black-screened. All three cards run at x8, because the CPU only has 28 lanes to hand out.

What I am aiming at

So far I have been running other people's models well. The next thing I want is to train them — properly, starting with fine-tuning on my own data and working toward building a language model from scratch, so that I understand the whole path rather than the half of it that happens after the weights exist.

Past that, more systems built with AI rather than merely using it. The interesting question is not what a model can answer, it is what a machine can be trusted to finish on its own.

Academically I am working toward a transfer to Georgia Tech, for computer engineering or AI engineering. Which is a strange thing to write down, because I have had my hands on graphics cards since I was eleven years old and none of it was ever a plan. It is funny how it worked out like this.
